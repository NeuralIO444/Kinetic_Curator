# Dish Friction Physics Research — Contact Friction, Spin, and Simple Constraints in Impulse-Based Disc/Circle Engines

## Summary

The production pattern is uniform and cheap to copy in miniature: solve contact **velocity** with a normal impulse first, then a tangential impulse whose *accumulated* magnitude is clamped to the Coulomb cone `|λt| ≤ μ·λn`. Static friction is not, in Box2D/Chipmunk, a separate force law — it is what the clamp produces when the impulse needed to cancel tangential contact velocity fits inside the cone; kinetic friction is what it produces when it saturates. Matter.js is the outlier: a position-then-velocity resolver with three loosely-coupled knobs (`friction`, `frictionStatic = multiplier on friction`, `frictionAir = per-step velocity decay, not a contact force at all`), and documented slope-creep that its maintainer attributes to friction/slop/mass/iteration balance.

The single most load-bearing warning for the target design — **single-pass, fixed-order, no persistent manifold, friction budgeted only from this step's normal impulse** — is that every production engine that rests stacks quietly does the opposite in at least one respect: it *accumulates* impulses within the step over several iterations, and it *warm-starts* from a persistent, ID-matched contact (Catto's arbiter/contact-ID coherence; Chipmunk's cached arbiters and `collisionPersistence`). Budgeting friction from a single-pass `jn` systematically under-funds friction for loaded/resting contacts and over-funds it for glancing ones, makes the result strongly order-dependent (last-solved pair wins, in Gauss–Seidel), and makes resting friction rebuild from zero every frame — i.e. creep, exactly the failure Matter users report even *with* iterations.

Determinism is nevertheless easier for this design than for a production engine, *if* it stays single-threaded, fixed-`dt`, fixed-order (sorted by stable pair ID, never hash/container order), with no randomized constraint order, no parallel atomic accumulation, and no hidden solver state (warm-start caches, sweep direction, sleep counters) that is not part of the checkpointed state. Bit-exactness *across* compilers/architectures/GPUs requires fixed-point/integer math (multiple projects converge on Q16.16–32.32 fixed point, sorted iteration, integer sqrt); float determinism on one binary/one machine is achievable and is what Gaffer/RaiSim actually claim.

For spin, the minimal honest model is one extra scalar per disc (ω, plus θ for rendering), `I = ½mR²` for a solid disc, contact-point velocity `v + ω×r`, and `Δω = (r × jt)/I` from the *same* clamped tangential impulse — no new budget. Rolling resistance is a separate effect (in pure rolling the contact point is instantaneously at rest, so Coulomb friction does no work there) and, if wanted, is a second tiny row opposing ω with its own clamp `μr·λn`. Faking spin visually (rotate θ by `vt/R` or by a stored spin field, with no back-reaction on velocity) is standard creative practice and is what Jakobsen himself does for limb axial rotation.

For aggregation (Q5): there is **no published source found** that directly ties contact friction → contact duration → pigment/mass exchange rate. What *is* published, and is strong, is the adjacent chain: tangential constraints (sliding/rolling/twisting, modified Coulomb) in colloidal-gel simulations drop the isostatic contact number from 6 to 2, shorten gel time at fixed volume fraction, and produce open DLCA-like clusters (`df ≈ 1.8`) instead of compact ones; conversely, lowering *sticking probability* in DLA/DLCA densifies clusters (`df` 1.7–1.8 → 1.9–2.2). Friction in a solver is, functionally, a sticking/dwell enhancer — so expect it to push morphology toward open, ramified, early-percolating aggregates and to raise any per-contact-time exchange integral. That last step is an inference to test, not a cited result.

Confidence: Q1, Q4 high (primary talks/manuals/source docs agree). Q2 high for threats, medium for specific fixed-point projects (several are small/new GitHub projects, used only as existence proofs). Q3 high for formulas, low for published mobile-cost numbers (no good primary benchmark found; cost argument is arithmetic, flagged as such). Q5 medium — solid DLCA/gel literature, no direct friction→duration→exchange paper found.

## Findings

### 1. Coulomb friction in sequential-impulse solvers

#### 1.1 The standard formulation

For a contact with unit normal `n`, tangent `t` (2D: `t = perp(n)`), contact offset `r_i` from each centre, the velocity that matters is the **contact-point** relative velocity, including spin (Catto, GDC 2005 iterative-dynamics slides, index):

- `v_rel = (v2 + ω2 × r2) − (v1 + ω1 × r1)`, `vn = v_rel·n`, `vt = v_rel·t`.

Sequential impulse (Catto, GDC 2006, index — https://box2d--org--0a07hml825e06.npsv6.com/files/ErinCatto_SequentialImpulses_GDC2006.pdf):

- Normal impulse increment chosen to drive `vn` to the bias/restitution target, divided by the normal effective mass `kn`; friction increment likewise with `kt`, where (2D scalar form of the slide's matrix) `kt = 1/m1 + 1/m2 + (r1×t)²/I1 + (r2×t)²/I2` — i.e. tangential impulses automatically create torque when spin exists, and spin automatically changes the tangential effective mass.
- **Accumulated clamping** is the core trick, not an optimisation: keep running totals `Pn, Pt` for the contact across all iterations of the step. Each iteration computes an increment, then `Pn ← max(Pn + ΔPn, 0)` and `Pt ← clamp(Pt + ΔPt, −μ·Pn, +μ·Pn)`, and applies only the *difference* from the old total. The slides explicitly contrast this with naïve per-iteration impulses, where each contact forgets its history, every impulse must be positive, a bad impulse cannot be undone, and the result is jitter, no static friction, and no stacking.
- Warm starting / coherence: at the next step, re-apply the old accumulated impulses as the initial guess. This requires matching old to new contacts, which Box2D-Lite does with feature-based **contact IDs** stored in an **arbiter** per touching pair. Fewer iterations are then needed and stability improves.

So the friction budget in the reference implementation is `μ × accumulated normal impulse for that contact, accumulated over iterations this step and seeded from last step` — not `μ ×` this instant's normal force, and not `μ × mg`.

#### 1.2 Static vs kinetic μ

- Textbook Coulomb: static friction is an inequality, `|fs| ≤ μs·N` (it supplies only as much as needed, up to the cap); kinetic is an equality while sliding, `fk = μk·N`, usually `μk < μs`. (Summarised in the Nature-of-Code-style force model as `F = −μ·|N|·v̂`, §1.5.)
- Box2D and Chipmunk collapse this to **one μ per pair**: if the tangential impulse required to zero `vt` lies inside `μ·Pn`, the contact sticks (that *is* static friction, with the correct magnitude automatically); if it saturates the clamp, the contact slides at the cone boundary (that *is* kinetic friction, with the same μ). There is no velocity-threshold switch and no separate `μs/μk` in the solver. A common cheap extension (not in Box2D) is a two-μ variant: use `μs` for the clamp when the pre-solve `|vt|` is below a small threshold and `μk` when above — expect a discontinuity/hunting at the threshold unless the switch is latched per contact, which again wants persistence.
- Mixing rules differ: Box2D's manual specifies the geometric mean, `μ = sqrt(μA·μB)` (so one zero kills friction) — index, https://github.com/Quantum-Fringe-Entertainment/Physics-Simulations-Box2D/raw/refs/heads/master/Box2D_v2.3.0_User_Manual.pdf. For the target design's *medium-averaged* per-pair coefficient (e.g. arithmetic mean of the two discs' μ through the intervening medium), note this is a deliberate departure: arithmetic mean does **not** have the zero-kills property, so a frictionless disc in a frictional medium will still grip. That may be artistically desired, but it should be a conscious choice, and geometric mean is the one-line alternative with production precedent.

#### 1.3 How the three engines differ

| | Box2D (Catto) | Chipmunk2D | Matter.js |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solver | Sequential impulse (projected Gauss–Seidel) over contacts+joints, iterated; split position/velocity in modern versions | Iterative impulse solver, cached **arbiters** per pair, contact persistence exposed as a space property (`collisionPersistence`, formerly a global — VERSION notes, index, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/slembcke/Chipmunk2D/master/VERSION.txt); arbiter API exposes total impulse *with friction* per pair (manual, index, https://github.com/vibloteket/munk2d/blob/HEAD/docs/manual.md) | Position iterations + velocity iterations + constraint iterations as separate passes over persistent **pairs**; not a unified accumulated-impulse LCP solve |
| Friction params | One `friction` per fixture, geometric-mean mixing, one cone clamp on accumulated `Pt` | One friction coefficient per shape, combined per arbiter; also `surface velocity` on shapes (conveyor behaviour falls out of the tangent solve) | Three separate knobs, defaults `friction 0.1`, `frictionStatic 0.5`, `frictionAir 0.01` (docs corpus, index, https://github.com/imfurman/phaser-docs-engineer/blob/HEAD/references/corpus/docs.phaser.io/api-documentation/typedef/types-physics-matter.md) |
| Static friction | Emergent from the cone clamp (see §1.2) | Emergent, same mechanism | `frictionStatic` is documented as a **multiplier on `friction`** used when the body is nearly stationary — a heuristic boost, not a second cone |
| Air friction | Not a body property (global damping exists separately) | Space damping (global) | `frictionAir` is a per-body, per-step **velocity decay factor** applied in integration — no contact, no area, no direction. The maintainer describes it in issue #404 as very simple, designed only to look reasonable in games (index, https://github.com/liabru/matter-js/issues/404) |

Matter's known simplifications / failure mode, documented in its own tracker: a box on a slope keeps sliding slowly no matter how large `friction` (1) and `frictionStatic` (10+) are set; the maintainer's reply is that friction has limits, is a balancing act with slop and mass, may need more iterations, and reopens it as a known issue (issue #752, index, https://github.com/liabru/matter-js/issues/752). Treat Matter's model as a cautionary example of what a decoupled, non-accumulated friction heuristic buys you: easy tuning for tumbling scenes, unreliable resting/sliding thresholds.

#### 1.4 What breaks when friction is budgeted ONLY by the current step's normal impulse (no manifold, no accumulation)

This is the target solver's stated design, so the failure list is specific:

1. **Loaded contacts are under-budgeted, in proportion to load depth.** In a resting stack/pile, the normal impulse a contact *should* carry grows with everything resting on it. An iterated, accumulated solver builds that up over iterations as impulses propagate through the pile within one step. A single pass in fixed order propagates load only as far as the ordering allows: pairs solved early see almost no normal impulse from pairs solved late, so their friction clamp is near zero for that step; pairs solved late inherit a larger `jn` — friction strength becomes a function of pair order, not of physics. Expect: bottom-of-pile discs grip, top-of-pile discs skate, and swapping the pair order visibly changes behaviour (cf. §2 on order as a determinism *and* quality issue).
2. **Resting friction restarts from zero every step.** Without warm starting, the tangential impulse that was holding a disc on an incline is discarded; each step the disc acquires a step's worth of tangential velocity under gravity before friction is re-derived, then (if the cone is big enough) is stopped again. Net effect is a steady creep velocity of order `g_t·dt` per step rather than rest — the Matter slope issue (§1.3) reproduced by construction. Sleeping/velocity thresholds or a latch are the usual patches; all add state.
3. **Bias/penetration impulses pollute or starve the budget, pick one.** If `jn` includes the positional (Baumgarte/split-impulse) correction, deep-but-light contacts get large friction budgets (sticky pops). If `jn` is velocity-only (the cleaner Box2D-style choice, where bias is kept out of the velocity state — Catto lists this as a known loose end), a contact that is merely *touching* with near-zero approach velocity gets `jn ≈ m·g·dt`-scale budget at best, and a contact discovered already separating gets none, even though it was loaded all step.
4. **No manifold = no torque balance and double-counting.** A disc–disc contact is a single point, which is fine — discs are the lucky case. But a disc resting in the V between two neighbours has two points whose friction must *cooperate*; single-pass Gauss–Seidel solves them independently, so the first can saturate its cone cancelling a `vt` that the second then re-creates. Iterated solvers converge out of this; a single pass leaves a residual tangential velocity every step, i.e. slow rolling/sliding drift in piles.
5. **Impact friction is over-budgeted relative to resting friction.** A fast head-on impact produces a huge one-step `jn`, hence a huge allowed `jt` — the solver will happily cancel all tangential velocity and dump the difference into spin (§3) in one step, while a gently resting contact can barely hold still. Real Coulomb friction has the same proportionality, but production engines soften the contrast via accumulation over substeps/iterations and restitution thresholds; a single pass exposes it raw. If the art wants "collisions are grippy, rests are slippery", that is this bug as a feature — name it as such.

Mitigations that stay within "single-pass, no manifold": (a) budget friction from `jn` **plus a load proxy** — e.g. accumulate a per-particle scalar "normal load this step" in a first cheap pass over all pairs (Jacobi-style, order-independent if summed in fixed order), then do the impulse pass with `|jt| ≤ μ·(jn_pair + share of load)`; (b) use a *resting-contact* budget `μ·m_eff·|g_n|·dt` when penetration > 0 and approach velocity ≈ 0; (c) carry exactly one scalar per particle (not per pair) of last-step tangential hold — a half-step toward persistence at O(N) memory. None of these are production-standard; all are strictly better than raw per-pair `μ·jn` for piles. Flagged as design recommendations, not cited practice.

#### 1.5 What engines without persistent contacts do instead

- **Jakobsen / Verlet particle systems** (Hitman; the ancestor of most creative-coding particle physics) — VERIFIED LIVE, https://github.com/krisives/advanced-character-physics : there is no normal impulse at all. Friction is faked from **penetration depth**: when a particle is projected out of an obstacle, its tangential velocity (implicit, `x − x_prev`) is reduced by an amount proportional to the penetration depth `dp`, with the proportion being the friction constant, implemented by moving `x_prev` tangentially; the reduction is clamped so tangential velocity never reverses (if it would, it is set to zero — that clamp is the static-friction analogue). Jakobsen himself notes better models should exist. Properties: friction scales with *how badly you penetrated this step*, so it is timestep- and speed-dependent, vanishes for a particle resting exactly on the surface with no penetration, and has no cone, no μ in the physical sense, and no coupling between neighbours' loads.
- **toxiclibs (VerletPhysics)** — the javadoc surface is position, weight, lock, bounds, a list of per-particle constraints, and `scaleVelocity` (index, https://shiffman.github.io/toxiclibs-javadocs/toxi/physics2d/VerletParticle2D.html). There is no angle, no contact object, and no friction coefficient; resting/sliding behaviour comes from (i) global/physics damping, (ii) ground-plane constraints in user code that scale the tangential component of `(x − x_prev)` on contact — i.e. exactly Jakobsen's trick, hand-rolled per sketch.
- **Nature of Code / Processing style** — friction is a **force**, applied in the force-accumulation phase before integration: direction `−v̂`, magnitude `μ·|N|`, with `|N|` usually just assumed (`mg`, or 1) because there is no contact solver to supply it (secondary notes summarising Shiffman's model, index, https://github.com/logancyang/blog-learning-automata/blob/HEAD/_posts/2020-03-13-nature-of-code-1.md). It therefore acts even in free flight if coded naïvely (textbook examples gate it on "in contact with surface"), can reverse velocity if `μ|N|dt/m > |v|` unless explicitly clamped to stop-at-zero, and its static case does not exist — a "resting" mover under a tangential force accelerates until the friction *force* balances, then jitters around zero rather than sticking.
- **Position-based dynamics generally**: friction is acknowledged as the awkward case for position projection — objects on slopes slide regardless of friction strength — and the published workaround in the PBD community is tangential *breakable distance constraints* (a zero-rest-length stick along the tangent, created on contact, broken when stretched past a μ-derived limit), i.e. re-inventing the cone as a constraint with a breaking threshold (forum discussion among PBD/Jakobsen practitioners, index, https://pybullet.org/Bullet/phpBB3/viewtopic.php?t=1118). That same thread is the source for the cleanest one-line summary in this area: friction and restitution are naturally **velocity-level** constraints, which is why velocity (impulse) solvers handle them well and position solvers struggle.

### 2. Determinism

#### 2.1 What threatens bit-exact determinism, specifically in a friction solver

- **Constraint/pair iteration order.** The canonical story is Gaffer on Games' deterministic-lockstep post (VERIFIED LIVE, https://gafferongames.com/post/deterministic_lockstep/): ODE randomised constraint processing order for stability, which alone desynchronised two otherwise identical simulations; fixing the solver's seed per frame restored same-machine determinism. In a Gauss–Seidel friction solve, order is not cosmetic — later constraints see velocities already modified by earlier ones, and the friction clamp makes the map non-linear (saturating contacts are order-sensitive in a way linear springs are not). Randomising order per iteration is a real stability technique suggested for stacks (GameDev thread, index, https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/717122-stacking-box-slightly-slide-to-one-side-in-my-physics-engine/), and the immediate objection raised in that thread is exactly determinism. Fixed order is the deterministic choice; its cost is a systematic bias (first/last pairs dominate) that must be accepted or averaged by alternating direction — and alternating direction is itself hidden state (next bullet).
- **Hidden solver state: warm starts, sweep direction, sleep, islands.** RaiSim's determinism documentation (index, https://github.com/raisimtech/raisim2lib/blob/HEAD/docs/sections/Determinism.rst) makes the general point concretely: its solver alternates forward/backward sweeps and flips after every solve, so a step's result depends on how many solves preceded it; replays must set the direction explicitly or checkpoint/restore it with the world. The same applies verbatim to warm-started accumulated impulses (Catto §1.1): the cache *is* state. A no-persistent-manifold solver has *less* hidden state — a genuine determinism advantage — provided it also has no sleep counters, no island caching, and no time-based early-outs.
- **Floating point itself.** Gaffer: bit-exact means a checksum of the whole physics state matches every frame; float behaviour differs across compilers, OSes, instruction sets, and even debug vs release optimisation, and fixed `dt` is a necessary precondition (both sources above; also https://gafferongames.com/post/floating_point_determinism/, index). Modern restatements agree: FMA contraction, instruction reordering, and parallel accumulation order each flip low bits, and a physics sim amplifies one flipped bit into a different world within seconds (SunaEngine README, index, https://github.com/darienbathalter/sunaengine — its demo makes the point by running the same pachinko with contact forces summed in ascending vs descending order and getting different final bins).
- **Parallel / atomic accumulation order.** Newton's deterministic-contacts work (index, https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/pull/3661) is a good checklist of what GPU determinism actually costs: stable fingerprint tie-breaking for reductions, radix sort with stable sub-keys so contact order doesn't depend on thread scheduling, and — for the one path that sums contributions across threads — int64 **fixed-point aggregation**, because integer addition is associative and float atomic addition is not. Any GPU version of the target solver that accumulates per-particle impulses with float atomics is non-deterministic *by construction*, regardless of pair order.
- **Variable iteration counts / early-outs.** If iteration count depends on CPU speed, residual thresholds, or island size, two runs of the "same" step can do different amounts of work. A single-pass solver with a fixed pair list sidesteps this entirely — one of the strongest arguments for the target design.
- **Hash/container iteration order, RNG.** Deterministic projects replace hash maps with sorted structures (BTreeMap ADR: ordered entity collections, fixed-timestep accumulator, tick-indexed inputs, fixed point deferred to the determinism phase — index, https://github.com/loci2d/loci2d/blob/HEAD/docs/adr/en/0007-deterministic-simulation-and-fixed-point.md), sort pairs by ID, sort contacts within a manifold by a stable key, and use a seeded integer RNG whose state is in the snapshot (Backtrace architecture doc, index, https://github.com/bravenraja/backtrace-physics/blob/HEAD/docs/architecture.md). Note Jakobsen's singularity fix — dislocating coincident particles *at random* (§4) — is a determinism trap if the RNG is not seeded and checkpointed.

#### 2.2 How deterministic projects handle friction specifically

No source found describes a special "deterministic friction" algorithm; friction inherits whatever the solver does, with two friction-specific caveats:

1. **The clamp is a branch on accumulated quantities**, so any non-determinism in `λn` (order, float summation) is *amplified* at the cone boundary: two runs can disagree on stick vs slide for a marginal contact, after which trajectories diverge macroscopically, not just in low bits. Marginal contacts are therefore the first place to look when hashes diverge, and a fixed-point `λn/λt` pair makes stick/slide decisions reproducible even when a float version would be "close enough".
2. **Fixed-point projects quantise everything friction touches**: state in 32.32 (Backtrace) or Q16.16 signed 32-bit (SunaEngine, which claims identical 256-bit state hashes on any GPU/driver/platform for the same scene+inputs), angles quantised to a table (4096 steps, Backtrace), integer Newton-iteration sqrt. The cost is range/precision engineering (overflow must be impossible by construction, per Newton PR) and losing `libm` transcendentals. For a 2D disc solver, the needed operations are `+ − × ÷ sqrt` and one normalisation per pair — an unusually good fit for fixed point.

#### 2.3 Single-pass (non-iterative) resolution with friction: published guidance and tradeoffs

There is no paper titled "single-pass friction for particle crowds" found; the guidance is assembled from the primary sources' own statements about iteration:

- Catto: naïve (single, unaccumulated) impulses → jitter, no static friction, no stacking; accumulation + iteration is presented as *the* fix, with iteration terminating on a fixed count or when impulses become small.
- Jakobsen: constraints are explicitly *not* guaranteed satisfied after one iteration; the system converges over several frames instead, and the residual stiffness/softness from stopping early is often visually desirable. Hitman used 1 iteration for plants (the softness *was* the look), 3–4 for rigid tetrahedra, and 1–10 depending on object (VERIFIED LIVE, §4 source). Inaccuracies are smallest when motion is slow — convenient, since fast motion masks them.
- XPBD / Small Steps (summarised in the phyz-xpbd README, index, https://github.com/ecto/phyz/blob/HEAD/crates/phyz-xpbd/README.md): for a fixed projection budget, **substepping beats iterating** — many small steps with 1 iteration each (their default is 10 substeps × 1 iteration) cut integration error as well as constraint error, and their measured 20-link chain converges toward the many-iteration answer as substeps/iterations increase. For the target instrument this is the most actionable result in this section: if one pass per frame is too soft/slippery, prefer 2–4 substeps × 1 pass over 1 step × 4 passes — same pair-solve count, better resting friction (each substep re-derives a friction budget from a smaller `g·dt`, so creep per §1.4.2 shrinks), and determinism is untouched (fixed substep count).
- Quality summary for single-pass crowds: pairwise collisions and sparse crowds — fine, errors are local and don't compound. Dense piles, stacks, and anything resting under load — the known weak case (§1.4): load and friction both propagate one pair per pass, so equilibration takes O(depth) *frames*, visible as slow settling, creeping, and order-striped friction. There is no published fix that keeps exactly one pass and no persistence; all published fixes spend either iterations, substeps, persistence, or a load pre-pass.

### 3. Spin / angular velocity for discs

#### 3.1 Minimal model (one scalar per disc)

State per disc: add `θ` (angle, for rendering/integration) and `ω` (scalar, z-component in 2D). Solid-disc inertia `I = ½·m·R²` (hollow ring: `m·R²`; use `I = κ·m·R²` with `κ` as a tunable "disc vs ring" art parameter if wanted — the formulas don't care).

- Contact-point velocity: `v_c = v + ω × r`, in 2D `ω × r = ω·(−r_y, r_x)`.
- Effective masses pick up the angular terms automatically (§1.1): `k_n` gains `(r×n)²/I`, `k_t` gains `(r×t)²/I` per body. **For disc–disc contacts this is unusually simple**: the contact point lies on the line of centres, so `r ∥ n`, hence `r×n = 0` (normal impulses never spin discs up, and spin never affects the normal solve) and `|r×t| = R` (tangential coupling is maximal and constant). A disc engine gets exact normal/tangent decoupling for free — polygons do not.
- Torque from friction: applying `jt·t` at offset `r` gives `Δω = (r × (jt·t))/I = ±R·jt/I` (sign from orientation). No separate torque pass is needed if impulses are applied as `applyImpulse(P, r)` that updates both `v += P/m` and `ω += (r×P)/I`, which is the standard rigid-body impulse application in Catto's solvers.
- Rolling without slipping (disc on a surface / another disc): `v_c,t = 0`, i.e. for a ground contact `v_t = −ω·R` up to sign convention. If the friction cone can supply the needed `jt`, the solver produces rolling automatically — do not add a separate "rolling constraint" for ordinary contacts.
- Surface velocity / treadmills: Chipmunk's surface-velocity and Matter-style conveyors are the same row with a *target* `vt` instead of 0: `Δjt` drives `v_rel,t` toward the surface's tangential velocity, still cone-clamped.

#### 3.2 Rolling resistance — a different physical effect, a second tiny row

In pure rolling the contact point is instantaneously at rest, so Coulomb (sliding) friction does no work there and cannot stop a rolling disc; real stopping comes from deformation, modelled as rolling resistance (gizmo engine note, index, https://github.com/bdrtr/gizmo/commit/089dd71d5c38477748bbcb9b8b4492ee63a140ce). Implementation there and in the suspension literature (§5) is a Coulomb-form row on **angular** velocity: an impulse opposing relative ω, clamped to `μr·λn` (note: `μr` dimensionless in this impulse form; in force/torque form it often carries a length — do not mix the two conventions). Billiard models are the worked creative example: contact-point velocity `u = v + ω×r_c` splits the step into sliding (kinetic friction accel `−μs·g·û` plus torque `dω/dt = (1/I)·(r_c × m·a)`, sphere `I = 2/5·mR²`), rolling (resistance `−μr·g·v̂`, ω slaved to the rolling constraint), and free spinning (vertical spin decays at its own rate); ball–ball tangential impulse capped by the normal impulse is what produces collision-induced spin transfer ("throw") (index, https://github.com/austinio7116/thumbycue/blob/HEAD/PLAN.md). Recommendation: add rolling resistance only if discs visibly roll forever; default `μr = 0`, one scalar row, applied after the friction row, budgeted from the same `λn`.

Also cheap and often sufficient: **angular damping** `ω *= (1 − c·dt)` per step (the rotational analogue of Matter's `frictionAir`) — unphysical, one multiply, kills infinite-spin artefacts, and does not pretend to be a contact effect.

#### 3.3 Faking spin visually, without angular dynamics

Established creative practice, in increasing order of honesty:

1. **θ from translation**: render angle `θ_vis += (v_t / R)·dt` at the dominant contact, or simply `θ_vis += |v|/R·dt` with sign from travel direction. Looks like rolling on the ground, wrong in piles (discs translate without rolling and vice versa), zero physics cost.
2. **Independent spin field**: per-particle `θ_vis, ω_vis` integrated trivially, `ω_vis` damped or noise-driven, never read by the solver (the standard particle-system "rotation + spin" attributes in creative-coding particle libraries, where rotation is a render property alongside size/alpha). Collisions do not change it — audiences rarely notice for small, round, textured discs; they notice immediately for discs with a strong asymmetric mark in a pile.
3. **Orientation from structure**: Jakobsen derives limb orientation for *rendering* from particle positions via cross products, and explicitly does not simulate rotation about the limb's own axis, because it isn't essential to the motion and skipping it is a large speed win (VERIFIED LIVE, §4 source). The disc analogue: derive `θ_vis` from the vector to a bonded neighbour (§4) or from the local velocity gradient/curl of neighbouring particles — piles then show plausible counter-rotation with no ω state at all.
4. **Shader-only**: rotate the disc's texture/highlight by a hash of particle ID + time, or offset a specular dot tangentially by velocity. Appropriate when discs are pigment/mass carriers and "spin" is really about showing local shear.

Warning that applies to 1–3: if spin is ever meant to *feed back* — into friction direction, into exchange (§5), into sound — the fake must be promoted to the real ω column, because fake θ and physical ω will disagree exactly in the resting contacts where it matters.

#### 3.4 Cost notes (thousands of particles)

No primary mobile benchmark for "disc solver + ω" was found; the following is arithmetic from the formulas above, flagged as such. Adding ω/θ is +2 floats/particle and, per contact, roughly: computing `v_c` (+4 mult/add), the `(r×t)²/I` term (for discs a precomputable constant `R²/I = 2/m` per body — so in practice *zero* extra per-contact cost for the effective mass), and impulse application (+2 mult for `Δω`). Against a per-contact cost already dominated by the pair test, sqrt/normalisation, and memory traffic, spin is a few percent, not a factor. The real cost drivers at thousands of particles remain broadphase/neighbour search and contact *count* in dense piles (spatial hashing keeps neighbour search near O(N) with strongly density-dependent constants; clustered distributions are the bad case — cf. GPU SPH survey figures, index, https://mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/17/9706). On GPU, the binding constraint is not ω arithmetic but §2.1: a Gauss–Seidel single pass is sequential in pair order; a parallel version must go Jacobi/graph-coloured, and graph colouring with a *fixed* colour order is the standard way to keep that deterministic (and changes the solver's character — Jacobi needs more passes for piles, §2.3).

### 4. Distance / spring constraints between particles (compound bodies)

#### 4.1 Jakobsen relaxation (Verlet, position-level)

The stick constraint `|x2 − x1| = L` is satisfied by projection (VERIFIED LIVE, https://github.com/krisives/advanced-character-physics — paraphrased formulas, not quoted code):

- Equal masses: `delta = x2 − x1; diff = (|delta| − L)/|delta|; x1 += 0.5·diff·delta; x2 −= 0.5·diff·delta`.
- Unequal masses: divide by `|delta|·(w1 + w2)` (`w = 1/m`) and move each particle by its own `w_i` share; `w = 0` pins a particle.
- All constraints are relaxed in a loop (Gauss–Seidel: each constraint sees the previous constraints' corrections in the same pass); iterations trade accuracy for speed with no instability cliff, 3–4 iterations sufficing for a rigid tetrahedron (4 particles, 6 sticks = a 3D rigid body; the 2D disc analogue is 3 particles / 3 sticks, or a centre particle + rim particles with spokes and rim sticks for a compound disc).
- A square-root-free variant (one Newton step for the sqrt, expanded about the rest length) reduces each constraint to one division; with one iteration it also *removes* stiffness, which Jakobsen used deliberately.
- **Soft constraints**: repair only a fixed fraction of the deviation per frame (his example: 60 → 80 → 90 → 95… toward a rest length of 100). The fraction *is* the stiffness, and it is iteration- and frame-rate-dependent — see §4.3.
- Articulation for free: sharing a particle between two bodies is a pin joint; sharing two is a hinge; inequality sticks (`|x2−x1| ≥ L` only) give angular limits and self-collision.

#### 4.2 Sequential-impulse distance joints (Box2D)

The same constraint at velocity level (Catto GDC 2009, index, https://box2d.org/files/ErinCatto_ModelingAndSolvingConstraints_GDC2009.pdf): error is the separation of the two anchor points, converted to a bias velocity via the constraint's effective-mass matrix `K`, and solved in the same sequential-impulse loop as contacts, with warm-started accumulated impulse like any other constraint. Box2D's distance joint adds **softness** as a physical spring-damper (joint def docs, index, http://docs.ros.org/en/kinetic/api/mvsim/html/structb2DistanceJointDef.html): `frequencyHz` sets the mass-spring frequency (0 disables softness = hard constraint), `dampingRatio` 0 = no damping, 1 = critical damping. Internally this becomes compliance/bias terms in the impulse solve rather than an explicit spring force, which is why it stays stable at spring constants where an explicit `F = −kx` force would explode. Rule of thumb from the API's design: keep soft-joint frequencies well below the step rate (a spring resolved at ≥ ~1/10 of `1/dt` stops behaving like the requested spring), and prefer raising solver iterations over raising frequency for rigidity.

#### 4.3 XPBD — the modern synthesis, and the stiffness warning

Plain PBD (§4.1) has the documented flaw that effective stiffness depends on iteration count and timestep in a non-linear way, so "stiffness 0.5" is not a material property. XPBD fixes it with a physical compliance `α = 1/k` and a per-constraint Lagrange multiplier accumulated within the substep: `α̃ = α/h²; Δλ = (−C − α̃·λ)/(Σ w_i|∇C_i|² + α̃); Δx_i = w_i·∇C_i·Δλ`, with `C = |x1−x2| − L` for a distance constraint (summarised with measured validation in the phyz-xpbd README, index, §2.3 source). Converged behaviour then depends on `α` alone; iterations/substeps only change how fast it converges. For the target instrument: if compound-body stiffness is an *artistic material parameter* that must mean the same thing after a solver change, adopt the XPBD form for the distance pass even inside an otherwise impulse-based engine; if stiffness is just a knob, Jakobsen's repair-fraction is cheaper and its iteration-dependence should be documented as part of the instrument's character.

#### 4.4 Stability rules of thumb and determinism implications

- Iterations: 1 = cloth/plants/soft look; 3–4 = rigid compound bodies (Jakobsen); production impulse engines default higher for stacks/joints and expose the count. Constraint *chains* propagate information one link per Gauss–Seidel pass — a chain of length N needs O(N) passes (or substeps) to feel rigid end-to-end in one frame; this is the same propagation limit as §1.4.1 for pile load.
- Order matters and is visible: in a chain relaxed left-to-right, the right end moves most. Fixed order = deterministic but asymmetric; alternating order per pass hides it but adds the §2.1 hidden-state obligation; symmetric application (compute both ends from the same snapshot, Jacobi) is order-independent but converges slower and can oscillate for stiff chains without relaxation factor < 1.
- Determinism checklist for this subsystem specifically: fixed constraint list order (by stable ID), fixed iteration/substep count (never residual-based early-out in deterministic mode), seeded RNG if coincident-particle dislocation is used, and — in XPBD — λ reset at substep start is *state within the step only*, so it adds no checkpoint burden, unlike warm-started joint impulses in Box2D-style solvers, which do.

### 5. Contact-duration side effects: friction, coalescence, and aggregation

#### 5.1 What the DLCA/DLA literature actually establishes

- **Sticking probability controls morphology.** DLCA = particles/clusters diffuse and stick irreversibly on *every* collision; RLCA = stick with a probability < 1 (Jungblut et al., PCCP 2019, VERIFIED LIVE, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6484677/). In 3D, DLCA aggregates are open, `df ≈ 1.7–1.8`; RLCA aggregates are more compact, `df ≈ 1.9–2.1` (also RSC review, index, https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2016/en/c5en00155b, which gives RLCA up to ~2.1–2.2). DLA experiments varying the sticking coefficient directly show the same direction: lowering `Kstick` (1.0 → 0.01) lets walkers penetrate past the outer branches into the interior, producing denser clusters with higher `df` (index, https://arXiv.org/pdf/1105.5558). Mechanism, in one sentence: **anything that makes a first encounter more likely to become a permanent bond freezes in the open, branchy structure; anything that lets encounters fail, slide, or rearrange lets the cluster compact.**
- **Rotational freedom compacts; rotational constraint opens.** Including clusters' rotational diffusion (rDLCA) *lowers* `df` below standard DLCA for non-interacting particles, while repulsion (which lowers bond-formation probability) raises `df` and increases aggregation times (Jungblut et al., above). Rotation/rearrangement is a compaction channel; suppressing it preserves porosity.
- **Tangential (friction/adhesion) constraints change gelation quantitatively** — the closest published analogue to "turning on solver friction" (Nature Communications 2023, VERIFIED LIVE, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38461-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=6b1c15a0-f6c5-4835-9b60-c3180bbae3d6): compare attraction-only contacts (particles in contact slide and rotate freely) with adhesive contacts that additionally constrain sliding, rolling, and twisting in a modified-Coulomb manner (their simulations use μ = 0 vs μ = 1 for the two cases). With tangential constraints: the isostatic contact number for mechanical stability falls from `Nc = 6` (frictionless, 3D) to `Nc = 2`; gel time at fixed volume fraction is *shorter* (power laws `tg ≈ 0.011·φ^−2.1` adhesive vs `0.021·φ^−3.7` attractive); and the network is open and ramified, `df ≈ 1.8` (DLCA-like), vs compact attractive clusters (`df ≈ 3` at short range). Constraint counting for granular packings agrees in 2D: adding sliding friction raises constraints per contact from 1 to 2 and drops the isostatic coordination from `Z = 4` to `Z = 3`; adding rolling too gives 3 constraints/contact and `Z = 2` (index, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.10860v1.pdf).
- **Rolling friction specifically** lowers the volume fraction needed for discontinuous shear thickening and shear jamming, restructures the frictional force network, and increases the velocity correlation length (hence viscosity) (Singh et al., PRL 2020 abstract, index, https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10996v1). I.e. rolling resistance doesn't just slow rotation — it lets *sparser* systems behave as connected solids.

#### 5.2 The contact-duration link — what is inference, and what to do with it

No publication was found that measures the chain *friction coefficient → mean contact duration → rate of a per-contact exchange process (pigment, mass, charge, heat)* in a colloidal/granular simulation. The chain is, however, mechanically forced in an impulse solver, and should be presented to the design team as a testable hypothesis, not a result:

1. In a cone-clamped solver, raising μ converts sliding contacts (which separate or roll away on their own schedule) into sticking contacts (which persist until the *normal* load is removed). Mean contact duration therefore rises with μ, with a knee at the μ where typical contacts stop saturating the cone — the same knee as the stick/slide transition in §1.2.
2. Tangential constraints also arrest the *restructuring* that would otherwise break or bury contacts (§5.1: adhesive gels percolate earlier, at lower coordination), so friction raises both the duration of individual contacts and the fraction of particles in some contact at any time.
3. If the instrument's exchange rule is any integral over contact time (per-step probability, per-step transfer ∝ contact load, diffusion across a shared boundary), total exchange rate inherits both increases — superlinearly in μ near the knee, saturating when essentially all contacts stick. Expect the *visible* side effect of "adding friction" in such an instrument to be faster colour/mass mixing and faster, more open aggregation — i.e. friction is secretly also an aggregation-rate and exchange-rate knob, and tuning it for feel will retune the chemistry.
4. Conversely, the §1.4 budget flaw becomes a *chemistry* flaw: single-pass `μ·jn` under-funds friction exactly in long-lived resting contacts (small per-step `jn`) and over-funds it in brief impacts (large `jn`) — so exchange coupled to contact duration will be biased toward fresh, violent collisions and against the quiet pile, which is the opposite of the physical trend in §5.1. If exchange matters, budget friction from a load/dwell proxy (§1.4 mitigations), not from instantaneous `jn`.

Suggested validation experiment for the instrument (cheap, deterministic-friendly): log, per μ in {0, 0.1, 0.3, 1.0}, the histogram of contact lifetimes in steps, mean coordination, cluster `df` (2D analogue: mass vs radius of gyration), and total exchange events — the literature predicts monotone lifetime/coordination effects, a knee in lifetime at the stick transition, and morphology moving toward the open/DLCA end as μ rises.

## Recommended formulations

Notation: per pair (a,b), unit normal `n` from a to b, `t = (−n_y, n_x)`, offsets `r_a, r_b` (for discs `r_a = +R_a·n`, `r_b = −R_b·n`), inverse masses `w_a, w_b`, inverse inertias `q_a = 1/I_a` with `I = ½·m·R²`, relative contact velocity `v_rel = (v_b + ω_b×r_b) − (v_a + ω_a×r_a)`.

1. **Normal impulse (per pair, single pass)**
   `kn = w_a + w_b` for disc–disc (angular term is zero, §3.1); 
   `jn = −(v_rel·n − v_bias) / kn`, clamped by accumulation *within the pass over that pair's own substeps only*: `jn ← max(jn, 0)`; apply `∓jn·n`. Keep `v_bias` (penetration/restitution) out of the friction budget below.
2. **Friction impulse (the one to copy)**
   `kt = w_a + w_b + q_a·R_a² + q_b·R_b²` (disc–disc simplification);
   `jt_want = −(v_rel·t − v_surf,t) / kt` (recompute `v_rel` after the normal impulse);
   `jt = clamp(jt_want, −μ_pair·jn_ref, +μ_pair·jn_ref)`;
   apply `∓jt·t` as an impulse at `r` (updates `v` and `ω` together).
   With `jn_ref = jn` this is the target design as specified — ship it knowing §1.4. Preferred upgrade, same cost class: `jn_ref = jn + load_share`, where `load_share` comes from a fixed-order Jacobi pre-pass summing `jn` per particle.
3. **Pair coefficient**: `μ_pair = sqrt(μ_a·μ_b)` (Box2D precedent) — or the design's medium average, e.g. `μ_pair = (μ_a + μ_b + 2·μ_medium)/4`-style blends, documented as non-Box2D. Use the *same* averaged coefficient for the cone, rolling resistance, and any dwell/exchange coupling, so the three stay consistent.
4. **Static/kinetic**: single μ (emergent stick/slide). If a `μs > μk` distinction is artistically required, latch it per *particle* for a few steps (hysteresis), not per pair per step — no manifold means no clean place to latch per contact.
5. **Rolling resistance (optional, default 0)**: `j_roll_want = −(ω_b − ω_a)/ (q_a + q_b)`-form row on relative spin, `|j_roll| ≤ μr·jn_ref`, applied as pure torque. Plus global angular damping as the cheap always-on safety.
6. **Spin integration**: semi-implicit, same integrator as linear: `ω` updated by impulses, `θ += ω·dt`. Fake-spin fallback (§3.3) only if ω is cut entirely.
7. **Distance constraint (compound bodies), Jakobsen form**: `delta = x_b − x_a; d = |delta|; diff = (d − L)/(d·(w_a + w_b)); x_a += w_a·diff·delta; x_b −= w_b·diff·delta`, times a stiffness fraction `s ∈ (0,1]` per pass — or the XPBD form (§4.3) if stiffness must be solver-independent. Fixed constraint order, fixed pass count; prefer substeps over passes (§2.3).
8. **Determinism profile (minimum)**: fixed `dt`; pairs generated and sorted by `(minID, maxID)`; constraints sorted by ID; no hash-map iteration in the step; no RNG in the step except a seeded, checkpointed integer RNG; single-threaded impulse accumulation (or Jacobi + fixed-order reduction); sleeping, if any, with counters in the snapshot. **Bit-exact across platforms (only if required)**: fixed point (Q16.16 or 32.32) for positions/velocities/impulses, integer sqrt, no transcendentals in the step.

## Pitfalls — single-pass, fixed-order, no-persistent-manifold solver, per-pair medium-averaged coefficients

1. **Friction starves in piles, feasts in impacts** (§1.4.1, §1.4.5): `μ·jn` from one pass is a proxy for instantaneous violence, not load. Resting aggregates will be systematically too slippery; collisions too grippy (and too spinny, via `jt → Δω`).
2. **Perpetual creep**: no warm start ⇒ resting tangential hold is rebuilt from zero each step ⇒ steady slide of order `g_t·dt`/step on any incline, and slow pile spreading. A velocity deadband hides it and creates a visible start/stop threshold — choose consciously.
3. **Order striping**: fixed order (required for determinism) means pairs solved last systematically win; in a pile this reads as directional bias (drift toward/away from the low-ID side). Alternating order breaks the bias but adds hidden state that must be checkpointed (§2.1); Jacobi pre-passes (§1.4) are the order-clean alternative.
4. **Medium-averaged μ breaks the zero rule**: arithmetic/medium averaging means no pair is ever truly frictionless, and a "slippery" disc in a "grippy" medium grips. It also makes μ a *three-body* property (a, b, medium), so changing the medium under a resting pile changes its friction retroactively with no contact event — expect piles to lock or release when the medium field changes.
5. **Stick/slide flicker at the cone boundary**: contacts near `|jt_want| ≈ μ·jn` will alternate stick/slide step to step (no persistence to latch them), producing buzz, audible/visible jitter, and — if exchange is coupled to sticking — flickering exchange. A small hysteresis band or a per-particle latch (§Recommended 4) is the fix; both add state.
6. **Disc decoupling hides a bug until it doesn't**: disc–disc normal impulses never create spin (`r ∥ n`), so a solver bug that drops the angular term in `kn` is invisible — until a disc–wall or disc–compound contact with `r ∦ n` is added and normal response goes subtly wrong. Keep the general `k` formulas, use the disc simplification only as a checked specialisation.
7. **Rolling is frictionless by default**: with `μr = 0` and no angular damping, a disc that starts rolling on a flat floor rolls forever, and frictionless-*normal* piles of discs roll out from under load like ball bearings even at high sliding μ — the most common "friction doesn't work" report for disc engines. Budget a small `μr` or angular damping from day one.
8. **Spin runaway from grippy impacts**: §1.4.5's over-budgeted impact `jt` converts tangential velocity into ω in one step; with `I = ½mR²`, `Δω = 2·jt/(mR)` is large for small discs. Clamp `|ω|` (a max-angular-velocity setting exists in production engines for exactly this reason) or small discs will strobe.
9. **Stiffness amnesia in constraints**: Jakobsen repair-fraction stiffness changes meaning if pass count, substep count, or `dt` ever changes (§4.3) — in a staged design, a "stage 2 tuning" can silently retune stage 1's compounds. XPBD compliance, or freezing the step profile, prevents it.
10. **Determinism leaks through the exits**: the solver can be perfectly ordered while the *scene* is not — pair generation from a spatial hash iterated in bucket order, contact lists built by parallel broadphase, or exchange/aggregation bookkeeping in a hash map will each reintroduce run-to-run divergence that looks exactly like a friction bug (§2.1). Audit iteration order of every container the step touches, not just the solver loop.
11. **Fixed-point range, if adopted**: impulses for a whole pile accumulate into few contacts at the bottom; `μ·jn` products are the first quantities to overflow a Q16.16 range. Size the format from the pile-bottom impulse, not the median contact (Newton PR's "overflow impossible by construction" standard, §2.1).
12. **Friction is a chemistry knob in disguise** (§5.2): any μ change retunes contact lifetimes, coordination, cluster openness, and per-contact exchange. Version μ changes like material changes, and re-baseline aggregation/exchange tests when μ moves.
